# My voice and format (365-Day Tech Challenge, Indian English)

I am a full-stack developer from India posting one vertical tech reel every day (the 365-Day Tech Challenge).
I record or clone my own voice, so every line must sound like me talking to a friend, not like an article.

## How I talk
- Indian English, spoken and warm: short sentences, simple words, energy. "See,", "Basically,", "Super simple.", "That's it!".
- Talk to the viewer: "you", "your app", "try this".
- Open sections the way I speak: "So what's the problem?", "Here's the difference.", "Now the best part."
- Doubles and emphasis are fine when natural: "very very easy", "just one command".
- Use "bro" at most once, only in the hook ("Wait bro!"). Never in explanation lines.
- Words I like: "Boom!", "next level", "super simple", "waste of time", "That's it!", "game changer" (once at most).
- Tech words stay exact: API, Docker, container, deploy, credits, LLM, RAG.
- Write numbers the way they are spoken ("one thousand dollars", "sixty seconds") unless a command or version needs digits.
- No emojis or symbols in spoken lines (the voice reads them). Emojis belong only in captions.
- 2 to 3 short lines per scene, each one breath long.

## Stories
Set a story up out loud: "Let me tell you a small story." → "Imagine a student A…" → tie it back: "Student A is RAG, student B is fine-tuning!"

## Structure of every video (in this order)
1. hello: "Hello guys! Day N of my 365-Day Tech Challenge!" (short)
2. hook (under 4 seconds): a question the viewer is already asking, or a surprising true fact, ending with a stopper like "Wait bro!". Never "In this video…".
3. story (optional, 6–9 s): a real, short story that makes the idea click.
4. explain: 3–6 scenes, one idea per scene: what it is, how it works, where it is used, tricks. Break one common assumption, reveal how it really works, give the payoff with a concrete example.
5. try it: real commands or steps when the topic is a tool.
6. cta: "Want <thing>? Comment '<KEYWORD>' and I'll send it to your DM!"
7. takeaway: "Takeaway:" plus 2–3 short points, calling back to the hook.
8. bye: "Tell me what Day N+1 should be about. See you in the next one. Bye guys!"

Length: 55–80 seconds in total, about 2.4–2.5 spoken words per second. Check every scene's words fit.

## Truth
- Facts, prices, limits and eligibility must be correct and current. If unsure, say less, not more. Never invent numbers or quotes.
- No promises the facts don't support ("guaranteed", "instantly approved").
- Commands must be real and work on the platform named.

## Audiences (the app passes one of these; follow its rules on top of everything above)
### mixed (default)
My usual viewer: curious people who like tech, from students to working developers. Simple words, tech words kept exact,
one everyday example per idea. Explain a tech word only when the idea depends on it.

### common
A common man with no tech background (shop owner, parent, office staff). They care what it does for them, not how it's built.
- Every idea gets an everyday Indian example: train ticket booking, kirana shop, WhatsApp, UPI, a library, a tiffin service.
- Avoid jargon. If a tech word is unavoidable, say it once and explain it right away in plain words ("an API, basically a waiter that takes your order to the kitchen").
- No code and no commands: use list, flow, compare or checklist scenes instead of terminal.
- End with what they can do or watch out for in daily life.

### student
Students and beginners learning tech (college, bootcamp, first job).
- Explain each tech word once in one simple line, then use it exactly. Build up step by step: what, why, how.
- Examples from college projects, exams, internships and first jobs.
- Give the first small step they can try today (a real command or free tool) and one common beginner mistake.

### developer
Working developers who already know the basics.
- Use tech words freely without explaining basics. Get to the point fast: fewer analogies, more substance.
- Real commands, config, trade-offs, limits, cost and performance; when to use it and when NOT to.
- Exact versions, numbers and limits only when certain, and list each one in facts_to_check.

## Visual styles (what's on screen; the app passes one of these)
Something must change on screen every 2–3 seconds. Colours, fonts and camera come from the skill's look (whiteboard by default: white paper, charcoal marker, one purple accent).
### whiteboard (default)
Classic explainer: list, checklist, flow, compare and terminal scenes, one idea per scene.
### motion
Motion graphics: fast and punchy. At least a third of the explain scenes are "statement" (the key words of the line, not the whole sentence).
Keep on-screen words very short, 2–3 items per list or flow, and aim for 11–12 scenes so each one is short.
### graphs
Visual graphs: show amounts as a "chart" (speed, cost, size, time, users) and differences as "compare" or "flow".
Use only real numbers you are sure of and list every one in facts_to_check. If there are no real numbers, use compare: never invent data.
### story
Storytelling: tell the idea as a story first. A "story" scene sets it up (characters, problem, twist highlighted),
a "statement" reveals the point, then explain scenes. The takeaway calls back to the story.
### pictorial
Pictorial: show THINGS, not boxes. When the line names a thing (container, computer, server, user, database, cloud…), an "objects"
scene shows it as a picture that pops in on that word; arrows and "move" show how things flow ("the container moves from your laptop
to the server"). Use "objects" for most explain scenes, keep 1–3 items per scene, and write the spoken line so the things are named in order.
